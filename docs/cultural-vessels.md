# Cultural vessel references

Checked online on 2026-11-09. These are stylised form studies, not measured
reconstructions or claims that a country has one definitive vessel shape.
The supplied drawings guide the composition; the sources below qualify names,
construction and cultural context. Decorative motifs are not authenticated or
copied in this grey-only pass.

## 13 · Colombian clay mug

Use Alessandro's newer, straighter mug outline rather than the original
belly-shaped silhouette. Keep a substantial wall and rounded handle.

[Artesanías de Colombia: Ráquira pottery techniques](https://artesaniasdecolombia.com.co/PortalAC/Noticia/colombia-artesanal-raquira-de-arcilla-a-objeto-artesanal_6273)
documents clay preparation, forming and decoration in this particular Colombian
pottery centre. It supports the material and craft context, **not the provenance
of the supplied mug, its exact profile, or its geometric motifs**. Do not label
this silhouette as an archaeological or specifically Muisca reconstruction.
Colombian ceramics include many regional traditions.

## 14 · Gaiwan, China

[Chitra Collection: The gaiwan](https://chitracollection.com/the-gaiwan-a-coveted-covered-cup/)
describes a lid, tea bowl and saucer as its three most common components.
[V&A: Lidded bowl and saucer, O34301](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O34301/lidded-bowl-and-unknown/)
also explicitly records a footed bowl, separate footed saucer and knobbed lid.

Draw a handleless, flared bowl with its own foot, saucer, domed lid and knob.
Add the saucer missing from the original eighteen-cup sheet. The selected dome
and flare are a stylisation, not the exact profile of the V&A's jade example.
Thin flat borders distinguish the stacked pieces without suggesting lighting.

## 15 · Yunomi, Japan

[Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art: Tea cup (yunomi-jawan), F1902.58](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm:fsg_F1902.58/)
records a Japanese stoneware teacup, 9.5 cm high and 8.4 cm in diameter.
[Yunomi: form and everyday use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yunomi)
describes the tall, handleless form and turned foot, distinguishing it from a
formal chawan.

Use an upright ceramic cup with a small foot and softened lower corners.
Do not present it as a wide matcha bowl. Heights here are artistic proportions,
not the Smithsonian object's measurements. Yunomi shapes vary.

## 16 · Kulhad / kulhar, India and South Asia

[Kulhar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kulhar) describes a handleless earthenware
cup, typically undecorated and unglazed.
[Pure Indian Foods: Clay kulhad](https://www.pureindianfoods.com/products/clay-kulhad-chai-tea-cup)
provides a contemporary physical example and explicitly identifies unglazed,
porous clay.

Draw an open, tapered cup with a narrower, substantial base and a modest lip.
No handle, lid or saucer. The bright painted examples supplied by the user are
stylistic suggestions, not evidence that traditional kulhads require decoration.
No claims about disposability or environmental impact are needed for this study.

## 17 · Çay glass, Türkiye and Azerbaijan

[UNESCO: Culture of Çay](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/culture-of-ay-tea-a-symbol-of-identity-hospitality-and-social-interaction-01685)
documents the shared tea cultures of Azerbaijan and Türkiye and specifically
describes pear-shaped drinking cups.

Use a rounded lower body, narrow waist, flared mouth and separate saucer, following
the supplied tea-glass outline. The saucer is part of that visual reference;
UNESCO's text is not a dimensional specification for it. The broad label
“Islamic glass” is not used: a religion does not define this single vessel form.
Related regional forms and names are not interchangeable in every context.
Glass remains opaque grey until a separate material study is requested.

## 18 · Glass with podstakannik, Russia

[Labytnangi Museum of Local History: Tea glass holder](https://ar.culture.ru/en/subject/podstakannik-9)
describes a cylindrical metal stand supporting a glass, with a handle that
protects the drinker's hand.

Make two distinct outlines: a taller open drinking glass and a shorter handled
holder around its lower portion, with a base and upper border. Keep the face on
the exposed glass above the holder. Do not turn this into a one-piece glass mug,
copy the museum object's political imagery, or infer a metal/glass finish from
the current opaque grey placeholder.
